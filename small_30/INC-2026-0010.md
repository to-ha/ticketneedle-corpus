## Symptom
The incident began with reports of the lifecycle policy deleting objects earlier than expected from the block storage volume snapshots. Initially, this was observed by the users who rely on these snapshots for data recovery and consistency. As the issue evolved, it became clear that the premature deletion was not isolated to a specific set of objects or users, indicating a systemic problem. The monitoring tools caught anomalies in the snapshot sizes and deletion patterns, but the exact cause remained elusive. Further investigation showed that the multipath configuration and the queue depth settings might be contributing factors, as the system was experiencing high latency and timeouts during snapshot operations.

The user impact was significant, with several critical workflows being interrupted due to the loss of expected data. The business relied heavily on the snapshots for compliance and backup purposes, making the timely resolution of this issue paramount. The monitoring system did catch some of the anomalies, specifically in terms of disk space usage and snapshot creation failures, but it did not provide a clear indication of the root cause. Tools like `multipath -ll` and `fio` were used to troubleshoot the issue, along with `rsync --dry-run` to verify the integrity of the snapshots without actually transferring data.

As the symptom complex unfolded, it became apparent that the problem was not just about premature deletions but also about the overall health and performance of the block storage system. The snapshots were not being created correctly, and when they were, they often failed to capture all the necessary data, leading to inconsistencies and further complications. This suggested that the issue might be related to how the system handled concurrent requests and managed its resources, particularly in terms of queue depth and service restarts.

The initial response to the symptom involved checking the system logs for any error messages related to the snapshot process and examining the configuration files for any recent changes. However, as the issue persisted, it became clear that a more thorough investigation was required, involving deeper dives into the system's performance metrics and possibly adjusting settings like max_queue_depth to optimize the system's ability to handle snapshot requests efficiently.

The evolution of the symptom over time indicated a need to look beyond the immediate error messages and consider the broader system context, including network connectivity, disk health, and the version of the multipath tools being used. Despite these efforts, pinpointing the exact cause proved challenging, necessitating a structured approach to diagnosis that would systematically rule out potential causes.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
Initially, the team suspected that the issue might be related to a recent software update, potentially introducing a bug that affected the lifecycle policy's execution. To investigate this, they reviewed the changelog of the update and checked for any known issues related to snapshot management. However, after analyzing the update's documentation and comparing it with the system's current configuration, they found no direct link between the update and the symptom.

Another hypothesis considered was that the network connectivity between the storage nodes might be unstable, causing interruptions in the snapshot process. The team used `tcpdump` to capture network traffic and analyze it for any signs of packet loss or corruption. Although some minor issues were found, they were not significant enough to cause the observed problems with the snapshots.

The team also explored the possibility of disk failures or corruption affecting the snapshot data. They ran diagnostics on the disks using `smartctl` and checked for any bad sectors, but the results showed no indication of hardware failure.

As part of the diagnostic process, the command ```multipath -ll | grep -c active``` was used to check the status of the multipath configuration, looking for any inactive paths that might be causing issues with data accessibility. This step provided valuable insights into the system's current state but did not directly point to the root cause.

Further investigation led to considering the hypothesis that the queue depth setting might be too low, causing the system to drop requests and thus leading to premature deletions. To test this, the team adjusted the max_queue_depth parameter in the multipath configuration file and monitored the system's behavior. While this change did improve the system's performance slightly, it did not fully resolve the issue, indicating that the root cause was more complex.

The actual root cause emerged after a thorough review of the system logs and configuration files, combined with the results from the diagnostic tests. It became clear that the block storage volume snapshot failure was due to a combination of factors, including insufficient queue depth and a misconfiguration in the snapshot timeout settings.

## Resolution steps
1. Restart multipathd service on node01
2. Set max_queue_depth to 64 in /etc/multipath.conf
3. Run fio --filename=/dev/mapper/mpath0 --size=10G --rw=randwrite
4. Verify snapshot size with rsync --dry-run -avz /mnt/src/ /mnt/dst/
5. Adjust snapshot timeout to 300 seconds in /etc/snapshot.conf

## Post-mortem note
The block storage volume snapshot failed due to insufficient queue depth. This root cause was initially missed because the focus was on the symptoms rather than the underlying system configuration. In hindsight, a more systematic approach to checking the system's settings and performance metrics could have led to a quicker resolution. Follow-up actions include scheduling a review of all system configurations to ensure that similar issues are prevented in the future and providing additional training to the support team on troubleshooting block storage systems. Furthermore, the incident highlighted the importance of proactive monitoring and the need for more granular alerts to catch performance issues before they escalate into critical failures.
