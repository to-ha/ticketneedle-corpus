## Symptom
The incident began with reports of service discovery returning stale endpoints after a deploy. This symptom was first observed by the development team, who were testing a new feature deployment. Initially, it seemed like an isolated issue, but as more teams started reporting similar problems, it became clear that this was a widespread problem affecting multiple services. The stale endpoints were causing errors and timeouts, leading to a significant impact on the user experience. Monitoring tools caught the issue through increased error rates and latency spikes in service discovery requests. However, the root cause of the stale endpoints was not immediately apparent, requiring further investigation.

As the incident evolved, more symptoms became apparent. Some services were unable to discover newly deployed endpoints, while others were stuck with outdated information. This led to a mix of errors, including connection timeouts, refused connections, and unexpected behavior from applications relying on service discovery. The development team tried to mitigate the issue by manually updating endpoint information, but this was not a scalable or reliable solution. The incident highlighted the critical role of service discovery in the platform infrastructure and the need for a robust and reliable DNS system.

The user and business impact of the incident was significant. Users experienced errors and delays when interacting with affected services, leading to frustration and a loss of trust in the platform. From a business perspective, the incident resulted in lost productivity and revenue, as well as potential long-term consequences if users choose to abandon the platform due to unreliability. The incident also underscored the importance of having a reliable and efficient service discovery mechanism, as it is a critical component of the platform's infrastructure.

Further analysis of the monitoring data revealed that the issue was not limited to a specific set of services or endpoints. The problem seemed to be systemic, affecting a wide range of services and deployments. This suggested that the root cause was likely related to the service discovery mechanism itself, rather than a specific application or deployment issue. The development team began to suspect that the issue might be related to the internal DNS system, which is responsible for resolving service names to endpoints.

The monitoring tools provided valuable insights into the issue, including error rates, latency metrics, and logs from the service discovery system. These metrics helped the team to identify patterns and correlations that would aid in diagnosing the root cause of the problem. For example, the error rates and latency spikes were closely correlated with the deployment of new services, suggesting that the issue was related to the way the service discovery system handled updates to endpoint information.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
The team considered several hypotheses to explain the stale endpoints issue. One initial suspicion was that the service discovery system was not properly configured to handle endpoint updates. To investigate this, the team reviewed the service discovery configuration files and logs, looking for any signs of misconfiguration or errors. However, after analyzing the configuration and logs, no evidence of misconfiguration was found, ruling out this hypothesis.

Another hypothesis was that the issue was related to network connectivity problems between services. The team used tools like `tcpdump` and `dig` to investigate network connectivity and DNS resolution. In particular, they used the following command to test DNS resolution:
```
dig +short example.service.internal
```
This command helped to confirm that DNS resolution was working correctly for some services, but not others. However, further analysis revealed that the network connectivity and DNS resolution were not the primary causes of the issue, as the problem persisted even after verifying that these components were functioning correctly.

The team also suspected that the issue might be related to the registry-gc tool, which is used to clean up stale records in the service discovery system. They ran the `registry-gc` tool with the `--dry-run` option to check for stale records, but found no evidence of issues with the tool itself. Additionally, they reviewed the flag-history and vault-style CLI logs to see if there were any issues with endpoint updates or record management. While these tools provided valuable insights, they did not reveal the root cause of the problem.

As the investigation continued, the team began to suspect that the issue might be related to the internal DNS cache. They reviewed the DNS cache configuration and logs, looking for any signs of issues or misconfiguration. After analyzing the cache configuration and logs, they found evidence suggesting that the cache was not properly configured, which could lead to stale endpoints being returned.

## Resolution steps
1. Restart DNS server with --dns-cache-size=1000
2. Set dns.cache.ttl=3600 in /etc/dns/config.yaml
3. Run registry-gc --dry-run to check for stale records
4. Remove expired records with vault-cli delete --path=/dns/records/expired
5. Update flag-history with --dns-server-version=1.2.3

## Post-mortem note
The internal DNS cache was not properly configured and exceeded its maximum size of 500 entries. This root cause was initially missed due to the complexity of the issue and the multitude of potential causes. In retrospect, the team should have focused more closely on the DNS cache configuration earlier in the investigation. To prevent similar incidents in the future, the team will review and update the DNS cache configuration to ensure it is properly sized and configured for the platform's needs. Additionally, the team will develop more robust monitoring and alerting for DNS cache issues to catch potential problems before they become incidents.
