# Major Incident — INC-2026-0030
* **Ticket ID:** INC-2026-0030
* **Reported:** 2026-02-09 06:16 UTC
* **Priority:** P2
* **Domain:** Storage
* **Status:** Resolved

## Symptom
The incident began with reports of objects being deleted earlier than expected by the lifecycle policy. This issue was first observed by the storage team at 06:00 UTC, when they noticed a significant increase in deletion requests. Initially, it was thought to be a minor glitch, but as the morning progressed, the problem persisted, and users started to report missing files. The storage monitoring system alerted the team to an unusual spike in delete operations, which prompted a deeper investigation. The symptom was peculiar because the lifecycle policy was designed to delete objects after a certain period, but in this case, objects were being deleted almost immediately after creation.

As the investigation continued, it became clear that the issue was not limited to a specific bucket or prefix. The deletion pattern seemed random, and the team struggled to identify a common factor among the affected objects. The user impact was significant, as critical data was being lost, and the business relied heavily on the storage system for daily operations. The monitoring system caught the initial spike in deletions, but it did not provide enough information to pinpoint the root cause. Further analysis was needed to understand the underlying issue.

The storage team worked closely with the development team to review the code and configuration of the lifecycle policy. They checked the policy settings, the bucket configurations, and the object metadata, but nothing seemed out of the ordinary. The team also reviewed the system logs, searching for any error messages or unusual patterns, but the logs did not reveal any obvious issues. As the day progressed, the team became increasingly concerned about the potential consequences of the incident and the need to find a solution quickly.

The incident highlighted the importance of monitoring and logging in identifying and resolving issues. The storage team realized that they needed to improve their monitoring capabilities to detect similar issues earlier and prevent data loss. The team also recognized the value of collaboration and communication in resolving complex incidents. By working together and sharing knowledge, they were able to gather more information and piece together the puzzle.

As the investigation continued, the team discovered that the issue was related to the distributed filesystem metadata server. The server was responsible for managing the metadata of the objects, including their creation and deletion timestamps. The team suspected that there might be an issue with the server's configuration or performance, but they needed more information to confirm their hypothesis.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
The team considered several hypotheses to explain the unusual deletion pattern. One possibility was that the lifecycle policy was misconfigured, causing it to delete objects prematurely. To investigate this hypothesis, the team reviewed the policy settings and compared them to the expected behavior. However, the settings seemed correct, and the policy was configured to delete objects after a certain period.

Another hypothesis was that there was an issue with the object metadata, causing the deletion timestamps to be incorrect. The team used the `multipath -ll` command to verify the path status of the objects and checked the metadata for any inconsistencies. However, the metadata seemed correct, and the paths were properly configured.
```
multipath -ll /dev/sda
```
The team also suspected that there might be a performance issue with the distributed filesystem metadata server, causing it to malfunction and delete objects randomly. To investigate this hypothesis, they checked the server's performance metrics and reviewed the system logs for any error messages. However, the server's performance seemed normal, and there were no error messages in the logs.

As the team continued to investigate, they considered the possibility that the issue was related to the replication factor of the metadata server. They suspected that the replication factor might be incorrectly configured, causing the server to delete objects prematurely. To investigate this hypothesis, they reviewed the server's configuration and checked the replication factor settings.

The team also used the `iostat` command to analyze the disk performance and identify any potential bottlenecks. They checked the disk usage, the read and write speeds, and the error rates, but everything seemed normal. The team was puzzled by the lack of evidence pointing to a specific root cause and decided to continue investigating.

After several hours of investigation, the team finally discovered the root cause of the issue. The distributed filesystem metadata server had experienced a failure due to an incorrectly configured replication factor. The team realized that they had missed this critical configuration setting in their initial review and that it was the key to resolving the incident.

## Resolution steps
1. Restart metadata server on node 3 with --force-reload flag
2. Set /etc/metadata/server.conf replication_factor to 3
3. Run multipath -ll to verify path status on all nodes
4. Execute s3-style CLI command put-object --bucket mybucket --key metadata/check to test write access

## Post-mortem note
The distributed filesystem metadata server experienced a failure due to an incorrectly configured replication factor. This issue was missed initially because the team focused on the lifecycle policy and object metadata, rather than the metadata server's configuration. In hindsight, the team should have checked the replication factor settings earlier, as it was a critical configuration parameter. The incident highlighted the importance of thorough configuration reviews and the need for better monitoring and logging to detect similar issues earlier. The team will review their procedures and implement changes to prevent similar incidents in the future.
