* **Ticket ID:** INC-2026-0002
* **Reported:** 2026-04-05 21:44 UTC
* **Priority:** P1
* **Domain:** Containers / Kubernetes
* **Status:** Resolved

## Symptom
The incident began with reports of pods not becoming Ready despite the Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA) scaling up. This symptom was first observed by monitoring tools at 21:40 UTC, which triggered an alert due to the discrepancy between expected and actual pod readiness. Initially, it seemed like a minor issue that could be resolved quickly, but as time progressed, the number of affected pods increased, indicating a more serious problem. The user impact was significant because the application relied on these pods for handling incoming requests, and their unavailability led to errors and timeouts for end-users. 

Further investigation revealed that the pods were consistently failing to become Ready due to issues related to the ingress controller. The monitoring system had caught the initial surge in scaling requests but missed the underlying cause of the pod readiness issue. It became clear that a deeper dive into the ingress controller's configuration and its interaction with etcd was necessary to understand why the pods were not transitioning to a Ready state.

As the incident evolved, the engineering team started to notice patterns in the affected pods, all of which were related to the ingress controller's management. The team suspected that there might be a configuration issue or a miscommunication between the ingress controller and etcd that was preventing the pods from becoming Ready. However, at this stage, the exact root cause was still unknown, and the team needed to perform further diagnostics.

The symptom of HPA scaling up without pods becoming Ready persisted for several hours, causing significant disruption to service. The team recognized the urgency of resolving the issue quickly to minimize user impact. They began to explore various hypotheses, including network connectivity issues, resource constraints, and configuration problems within the Kubernetes cluster.

Despite the initial focus on pod-level issues, it became apparent that the problem was more complex and involved the interaction between several components of the Kubernetes ecosystem. The team decided to broaden their investigation to include the ingress controller, etcd, and the overall cluster configuration to find the root cause of the issue.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
The team initially suspected a network connectivity issue between the pods and the ingress controller. They attempted to diagnose this by checking the network policies and the pod's network interface configurations. However, after running `kubectl get networkpolicies` and examining the output, they found no issues that would prevent communication.

Next, they considered the hypothesis that resource constraints might be preventing pods from becoming Ready. This led them to investigate node resource utilization with `kubectl top nodes`, but they found that resources were not exhausted, ruling out this hypothesis.

Another hypothesis was that there might be a configuration issue within the Kubernetes cluster itself, potentially related to the ingress controller's setup. To explore this, they ran the command:
```
etcdctl get /ingress/config
```
This command provided insight into the current configuration stored in etcd, which seemed to match the expected settings, initially suggesting that the issue might not be with the etcd configuration.

The team also suspected an issue with the helm chart used for deploying the ingress controller. They attempted to verify the chart's version and configuration using `helm list` and `helm show values`, but these checks did not reveal any obvious problems.

Further investigation led them to consider the possibility of a miscommunication or synchronization issue between the ingress controller and etcd. This hypothesis seemed plausible given the symptoms, but diagnosing it required deeper inspection of logs and potential configuration mismatches.

After exploring several hypotheses and gathering evidence, the team began to suspect that the root cause might be related to how the ingress controller's configuration was being synced with etcd. This realization came after a detailed review of the logs and configurations, which hinted at a potential mismatch or synchronization issue.

## Resolution steps
1. Restart ingress controller with --v=5 flag
2. Set etcd key /ingress/config/max-workers to 4
3. Update helm chart with new ingress config values
4. Verify etcdctl get /ingress/config returns updated values

## Post-mortem note
Ingress controller configuration was not properly synced with etcd. This root cause was initially missed due to the complexity of the interactions between the ingress controller, etcd, and the pods. The resolution steps effectively addressed the synchronization issue, ensuring that the ingress controller's configuration was properly reflected in etcd, thus resolving the pod readiness problem. Follow-up actions include reviewing the automation scripts for deploying and configuring the ingress controller to prevent similar synchronization issues in the future. Additionally, the team plans to enhance monitoring to catch etcd synchronization problems earlier, reducing the time to detect and resolve such incidents.
