## Symptom
The incident began with reports of autovacuum canceling under sustained write load on the read-replica pair. Initially, this was observed as occasional timeouts in the application logs, which were not immediately alarming due to their sporadic nature. However, as the day progressed, these timeouts became more frequent and were accompanied by noticeable increases in query latency. Monitoring caught the rising trend in query execution times and alerted the team, but the initial thresholds were set too high to trigger an immediate response to the autovacuum cancellations themselves.

As the situation evolved, database administrators noticed that the write load was not unusually high, suggesting that the issue might not be purely a matter of demand exceeding capacity. Instead, there seemed to be an inefficiency or bottleneck within the database's handling of these writes, particularly affecting the replica's ability to keep up with the primary server. This hypothesis was supported by observations from `pg_stat_activity`, which showed a buildup of pending writes on the replica.

The user and business impact became more pronounced as the incident continued. Users began reporting errors when attempting to perform operations that relied on up-to-date data, highlighting the consequences of the read-replica lag. Internally, the database team faced pressure to resolve the issue quickly to prevent further disruption to dependent services. Despite the focus on resolving the immediate problem, there was also a recognition of the need to understand why monitoring had not caught the issue sooner, particularly given that the autovacuum cancellations were a known potential problem under heavy write loads.

Further investigation into the monitoring setup revealed that while it was effective at detecting outright failures and certain types of performance degradation, it lacked the granularity needed to identify subtle issues like the one at hand. Specifically, the metrics related to autovacuum and replica lag were not being closely monitored in real-time, leading to a delay in detecting the problem. This realization would later inform discussions about enhancing monitoring capabilities to prevent similar incidents.

The initial response to the incident involved a closer examination of system resources and database configuration. Tools like `iostat` and `pidstat` were used to assess disk I/O and CPU utilization, respectively, to determine if there were any obvious bottlenecks. However, these initial checks did not reveal any clear issues, suggesting that the problem might be more nuanced.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
Initial suspicions centered around a possible misconfiguration of the database or an unforeseen side effect of recent changes. The team considered the hypothesis that the write load had exceeded the capacity of the replica server, leading to lag. To investigate this, they examined disk I/O patterns using `iostat` and found that while there was significant activity, it did not appear to be the primary bottleneck.

Another hypothesis was that the autovacuum process was not properly tuned for the current workload, leading to frequent cancellations under pressure. However, after adjusting autovacuum settings and observing no improvement, this hypothesis was ruled out. The team also suspected issues with the network connectivity between the primary and replica servers but found no evidence of packet loss or significant latency after running `ping` and `tcpdump` tests.

To better understand the system's performance, the team executed the following command to monitor disk usage in real-time:
```
iostat -xd 5
```
This provided valuable insights into how the database was utilizing disk resources but did not directly point to the root cause of the replica lag.

Further investigation led to considering hypotheses around resource allocation and database configuration. The team suspected that perhaps the shared buffers were not adequately sized, leading to inefficient data handling. They also explored the idea that the effective cache size might be too small, causing the database to rely too heavily on disk I/O. However, it wasn't until they dove deeper into the specifics of the database's resource allocation and configuration that they began to uncover clues pointing towards the actual root cause.

The final hypothesis to be considered was that the database's settings for handling writes and replication were not optimal for the current workload. This led to a closer examination of settings such as `wal_sender_timeout` and `effective_cache_size`, which ultimately contributed to understanding the root cause of the issue.

## Resolution steps
1. Restart replica server with --max_connections=500
2. Set shared_buffers to 4GB in postgresql.conf
3. Run VACUUM on tables with bloat_ratio > 2.0
4. Adjust wal_sender_timeout to 60 seconds
5. Increase effective_cache_size to 8GB

## Post-mortem note
The read-replica pair experienced severe lag due to inadequate resource allocation. This incident was initially missed by monitoring due to a lack of granular metrics around autovacuum activity and replica lag. The eventual resolution involved a combination of adjustments to database settings and a better understanding of how the current workload was impacting the database's performance. In retrospect, the issue could have been caught earlier with more targeted monitoring and a deeper initial investigation into database configuration and resource utilization. Follow-up actions include enhancing monitoring to detect similar issues proactively and conducting a review of database settings to ensure they are optimized for the expected workload. Additionally, the team plans to implement more automated checks and alerts for potential bottlenecks in resource allocation and database performance to prevent future incidents of this nature.
