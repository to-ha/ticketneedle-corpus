## Symptom

The incident began with a specific deployment entering CrashLoopBackOff within 30 seconds of pod start. Pod logs showed a single line and then the container exited with code 137, indicating that the container was terminated by the system. This pattern was observed consistently across multiple pods in the deployment, suggesting a systematic issue rather than an isolated problem. As the rolling kernel upgrade progressed, more nodes began to exhibit this behavior, leading to a significant increase in the number of unavailable pods. The monitoring system caught this anomaly and alerted the team, but the initial alerts were not conclusive, and it took some time to understand the scope of the issue.

The symptom complex was characterized by a rapid sequence of pod restarts, with each pod failing shortly after startup. This created a challenging situation for debugging, as the pods were not running long enough to provide detailed logs or allow for interactive debugging. The team relied on the monitoring system and log aggregation tools to gather information about the failing pods. The shared symptom of pods entering CrashLoopBackOff within a short time frame after startup was a key indicator of the underlying problem.

Further analysis revealed that the nodes entering NotReady during the rolling kernel upgrade were experiencing difficulties with the etcd cluster. The etcd cluster is critical for maintaining the state of the Kubernetes cluster, and any issues with it can have far-reaching consequences. The team suspected that the problem might be related to the etcd cluster, but the initial evidence was not conclusive.

As the incident evolved, the team noticed that the pods were failing consistently, with a specific error message indicating that the liveness probe had failed. This suggested that the issue might be related to the configuration of the liveness probe or the startup process of the containers. The team began to investigate the configuration of the liveness probe and the startup process of the containers to understand why the pods were failing so quickly.

The user and business impact of the incident was significant, as the unavailable pods were part of a critical service. The service was not fully functional, and users were experiencing errors and delays. The team worked urgently to resolve the issue and restore the service to its full capacity.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses

The team considered several hypotheses to explain the observed behavior. One of the initial hypotheses was that the etcd cluster was experiencing issues due to a network connectivity problem. The team tried to verify this hypothesis by checking the network connections between the nodes, but they found no evidence of any issues.

Another hypothesis was that the liveness probe was not properly configured, leading to premature termination of the containers. The team investigated this hypothesis by reviewing the configuration of the liveness probe and checking the logs for any error messages related to the probe. They found some evidence suggesting that the probe might be too aggressive, but they needed more information to confirm this.

The team also suspected that the issue might be related to the startup process of the containers. They tried to verify this hypothesis by checking the logs for any error messages related to the startup process, but they found no conclusive evidence.

As the team continued to investigate, they considered the hypothesis that the issue might be related to the kernel upgrade itself. They checked the logs for any error messages related to the upgrade, but they found no evidence of any issues.

The team then tried to gather more information about the liveness probe and its configuration. They used the following command to describe the pod and grep for the liveness probe configuration:
```
kubectl describe pod -n default | grep Liveness
```
This command provided valuable information about the liveness probe configuration, which helped the team to understand why the pods were failing so quickly.

After analyzing the output of the command, the team realized that the liveness probe interval was too short, causing the containers to be terminated prematurely. This led them to suspect that the issue might be related to the configuration of the liveness probe.

## Resolution steps

1. Set initialDelaySeconds on liveness probe to 60s
2. Add separate startupProbe with failureThreshold 3
3. Update deployment config with kustomize edit
4. Apply new config with kubectl apply -f deploy.yaml
5. Verify etcd cluster stability with etcdctl member list

## Post-mortem note

Liveness probe interval was shorter than container startup time, causing premature kill. This root cause was not immediately apparent, and it took some time for the team to understand the issue. The team should have checked the liveness probe configuration earlier in the investigation. To prevent similar incidents in the future, the team will review the configuration of the liveness probe and ensure that it is properly set up. Additionally, the team will consider implementing more robust monitoring and alerting mechanisms to catch similar issues earlier. The incident highlighted the importance of carefully configuring the liveness probe and understanding its impact on the startup process of containers.
