## Symptom
The incident began with a sudden spike in deadlocks during a release window, which was first observed by the monitoring team at 13:25 UTC. Initially, the deadlocks were intermittent and did not seem to have a significant impact on user experience. However, as the release window progressed, the frequency and duration of the deadlocks increased, causing noticeable delays and errors for users. The monitoring system caught the spike in deadlocks and alerted the L1 Database operator, who began investigating the issue. Further analysis using pidstat and iostat revealed that disk I/O usage was higher than usual, which suggested that the issue might be related to disk performance or database maintenance.

As the incident evolved, the deadlocks became more frequent and prolonged, causing users to experience errors and timeouts. The L1 Database operator, Liu, attempted to troubleshoot the issue by checking database logs and system metrics but was unable to identify the root cause. The issue was then escalated to the L2 Database engineer, Ahmed, who began analyzing database performance metrics and querying system tables to understand the deadlock patterns. Despite these efforts, the deadlocks continued to occur, and the incident was further escalated to the L3 Database lead, Imani.

The user impact of the incident was significant, with many users experiencing errors and timeouts. The business impact was also substantial, as the release window was delayed, and critical updates were not applied on time. The monitoring system played a crucial role in detecting the issue, but it did not provide enough information to identify the root cause. Further analysis was needed to understand the underlying issue and resolve the incident.

The symptoms observed during the incident were consistent with a database maintenance issue, possibly related to vacuum or autovacuum daemon. The high disk I/O usage and frequent deadlocks suggested that the database was experiencing performance issues, which could be related to inadequate maintenance resources. To better understand the issue, the team decided to investigate the autovacuum daemon and its configuration.

The investigation revealed that the autovacuum daemon was not running as expected, and the database was not being properly maintained. This led to a hypothesis that the root cause of the incident might be related to insufficient maintenance resources. Further analysis was needed to confirm this hypothesis and identify the underlying issue.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
The team considered several hypotheses to explain the deadlocks, including inadequate disk performance, insufficient database maintenance, and incorrect database configuration. To investigate these hypotheses, the team used various diagnostic tools, such as pidstat, iostat, and psql equivalent client.

One hypothesis was that the deadlocks were caused by inadequate disk performance. To investigate this, the team analyzed disk I/O usage and found that it was higher than usual. However, further analysis revealed that the disk performance was not the primary cause of the issue.

Another hypothesis was that the deadlocks were caused by insufficient database maintenance. To investigate this, the team checked the autovacuum daemon configuration and found that it was not running as expected. The team then ran the following command to check the autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor:
```
psql -c "SHOW autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor;"
```
The output revealed that the autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor was set too low, which could be causing the insufficient maintenance resources.

The team also considered a hypothesis that the deadlocks were caused by incorrect database configuration. To investigate this, the team analyzed database configuration files and found that they were correctly configured.

After ruling out these hypotheses, the team discovered that the root cause of the incident was related to insufficient maintenance resources. The autovacuum daemon was not running as expected, and the database was not being properly maintained.

## Resolution steps
1. Set autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor to 0.1
2. Restart autovacuum daemon with -d option
3. Run VACUUM on pg_catalog tables with verbose output
4. Adjust shared_buffers to 128MB in postgresql.conf
5. Set maintenance_work_mem to 64MB

## Post-mortem note
The autovacuum daemon failed to clean up dead tuples due to insufficient maintenance resources. This issue was missed initially because the monitoring system did not provide enough information to identify the root cause. In hindsight, the team should have investigated the autovacuum daemon configuration earlier and adjusted the maintenance resources accordingly. To prevent similar incidents in the future, the team will review and adjust the database maintenance configuration and ensure that the autovacuum daemon is running as expected. Additionally, the team will implement more detailed monitoring to detect similar issues earlier and reduce the impact on users.
