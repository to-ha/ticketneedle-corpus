## Symptom
The incident began with reports of autovacuum cancellations under sustained write load, which initially seemed like an isolated issue but soon escalated into a broader problem affecting database performance. As the write load continued to increase, the autovacuum process was consistently cancelled, leading to a buildup of dead tuples and significant bloat in certain tables. This, in turn, caused query performance to degrade, with some queries taking significantly longer than usual to complete. The monitoring system caught the initial spike in write load and the subsequent cancellation of autovacuum processes, but it did not immediately alert on the broader performance impact, leading to a delay in escalation.

As the issue persisted, users began to report timeouts and errors when attempting to execute queries, further exacerbating the situation. The database team was alerted to the problem through a combination of monitoring alerts and user reports. Upon initial investigation, it seemed that the database was experiencing a classic case of transaction ID wraparound, but deeper analysis was required to confirm this hypothesis. The team started by examining the database logs for any signs of error or unusual activity, which revealed the consistent cancellation of autovacuum processes.

The symptom complex was characterized by a significant increase in dead tuples, table bloat, and degraded query performance, all of which were directly tied to the sustained write load and the autovacuum cancellations. As the team delved deeper into the issue, they began to suspect that the problem might be related to the WAL archive process, given the nature of the symptoms and the fact that autovacuum was being cancelled under write load. However, this hypothesis required further testing and analysis to confirm.

The monitoring system played a crucial role in identifying the initial symptoms, but it was not fully equipped to handle the nuances of this particular issue. The team had to resort to manual analysis of database logs and performance metrics to get a clearer picture of what was happening. This involved using tools like `iostat` to examine disk usage patterns, `pidstat` to monitor process activity, and `explain analyze` to understand query execution plans.

As the investigation continued, the team began to notice a correlation between the write load, autovacuum cancellations, and the performance degradation. It became clear that the issue was not just a simple matter of transaction ID wraparound, but rather a more complex problem involving the interaction between the database's write load, autovacuum process, and the WAL archive mechanism.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
The team initially suspected that the issue might be due to a simple misconfiguration of the autovacuum process, leading to its cancellation under write load. However, after reviewing the configuration files and database logs, this hypothesis was ruled out. The autovacuum settings were found to be appropriate for the workload, and there were no signs of misconfiguration.

Another hypothesis considered was that the disk subsystem was the bottleneck, causing the write load to back up and lead to autovacuum cancellations. To test this, the team used `iostat` to examine disk usage patterns, but the results did not support this hypothesis. Disk utilization was within expected ranges, and there were no signs of significant bottlenecks.

The team also explored the possibility that the issue was related to query performance, perhaps due to poorly optimized queries or inadequate indexing. However, after analyzing query execution plans using `explain analyze`, it became clear that query performance was not the primary cause of the problem.

As the investigation continued, the team began to focus more closely on the WAL archive process, given its critical role in managing transaction logs and ensuring database consistency. To better understand the behavior of the WAL archiver, the team executed the following command:
```
iostat -x 10 /dev/sda1
```
This provided valuable insights into the disk activity related to the WAL archive process, helping to inform the next steps in the diagnosis.

Further analysis revealed that the WAL archive process was indeed struggling to keep up with the transaction volume, leading to a backlog of unarchived logs and subsequent performance degradation. This realization brought the team closer to identifying the root cause of the issue.

## Resolution steps
1. Increase wal_sender_timeout to 60 seconds
2. Set archive_timeout to 30 minutes
3. Restart wal archiver process with -o flag
4. Verify archive_command points to /mnt/archive
5. Adjust iostat interval to 10 seconds for monitoring

## Post-mortem note
The WAL archive process was unable to keep up with transaction volume. This root cause was not immediately apparent, as the initial symptoms seemed to point towards a different issue. However, through a methodical process of hypothesis testing and evidence collection, the team was able to identify the true cause of the problem and implement effective resolutions. In retrospect, it's clear that the monitoring system, while useful, did not fully capture the nuances of this issue, highlighting the importance of detailed manual analysis in complex database performance problems. Future improvements to the monitoring system and the addition of more targeted alerts could help mitigate similar issues in the future.
