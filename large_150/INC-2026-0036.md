## Symptom
The incident began with reports of service discovery returning stale endpoints after a deploy, which led to a cascade of failures across dependent services. This symptom first appeared as intermittent errors in the application logs around 09:20 UTC but quickly escalated into a full-blown outage affecting all users. The monitoring system caught the initial spike in errors and alerted the on-call team, who immediately began investigating the cause. However, the root cause proved elusive, and the issue persisted for several hours, causing significant disruption to business operations. As the incident evolved, it became clear that the problem was not with the deploy process itself but rather with how services were being discovered and routed after the update.

Further investigation revealed that the stale endpoints were not being updated in a timely manner, leading to requests being sent to incorrect or non-existent service instances. This behavior was consistent across all services that relied on the internal DNS for service discovery, suggesting a problem with the DNS system itself. The team observed that the issue seemed to resolve temporarily when services were manually restarted or when traffic was rerouted, but these workarounds were not sustainable and did not address the underlying problem.

The user impact was significant, with all external and internal users experiencing errors when attempting to access services. The business impact was also substantial, with critical operations halted due to the inability to reliably route requests to the correct service endpoints. Despite the monitoring system's ability to detect the initial error spike, it missed the underlying DNS issue, highlighting a gap in the current monitoring strategy.

As the team dug deeper, they utilized tools like vault-style CLI for secure access, flag-history to understand recent configuration changes, and runner-logs to trace the execution paths of services. These tools provided valuable insights into the system's behavior but did not immediately reveal the root cause of the stale endpoints.

The evolution of the symptom over time suggested a complex interplay between service discovery, DNS updates, and traffic routing. The fact that manual interventions could temporarily alleviate the issue pointed towards a configuration or caching problem rather than a fundamental flaw in the service discovery mechanism itself.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
Initially, the team suspected a problem with the deploy process itself, thinking that perhaps the new service versions were not correctly registered with the service discovery mechanism. To investigate this hypothesis, they reviewed the deploy logs and checked for any errors during the registration process. However, no issues were found, ruling out problems with the deploy process.

Next, they considered the possibility of a network connectivity issue preventing services from correctly registering their endpoints. Diagnostic steps included running network tests and checking firewall rules, but all indications were that network connectivity was not the problem.

The team also explored the hypothesis that the service discovery mechanism was faulty, perhaps due to a bug or incorrect configuration. They attempted to reproduce the issue in a controlled environment and reviewed the service discovery logs for any anomalies. While this exercise provided valuable insights into how services were being discovered, it did not reveal the root cause of the stale endpoints.

Another hypothesis was that the DNS system was not updating correctly due to a caching issue. To diagnose this, they ran the command:
```
dig +short dns-cache-status.example.com
```
This command provided insight into the DNS cache status but did not immediately point to a caching issue as the root cause.

Further investigation led to considering a hypothesis involving misconfiguration of the internal DNS system, specifically regarding how TTL (time to live) values were set for cached records. The team reviewed the DNS configuration files and realized that the TTL value might be set too high, leading to stale records being served for an extended period.

After ruling out several hypotheses and gathering more evidence, the actual root cause began to emerge. It became clear that the issue was indeed related to the internal DNS system's configuration, specifically how it handled caching and updates of service endpoints.

## Resolution steps
1. Restart dns-cache service on node 3
2. Set dns_cache_ttl to 300 in /etc/dns/config.yaml
3. Update dns-updater script to use --retry-flag 5
4. Clear /var/lib/dns/cache directory

## Post-mortem note
The internal DNS cache expired due to a misconfigured ttl value. This root cause was initially missed because the team focused on symptoms rather than the underlying DNS configuration issue. In retrospect, a more thorough review of the DNS system's configuration and caching behavior would have led to a quicker resolution. Follow-up actions include reviewing and updating the monitoring strategy to better detect DNS-related issues, providing additional training to the team on DNS configuration and troubleshooting, and implementing automated checks for common DNS misconfigurations to prevent similar incidents in the future.
