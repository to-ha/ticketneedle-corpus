## Symptom
The incident began with reports of intermittent packet loss on a single uplink, which was first observed by monitoring tools at 05:25 UTC. This symptom was particularly noticeable during periods of high network traffic, suggesting a potential issue with the uplink's capacity to handle the load. Further investigation revealed that the packet loss was not consistent and would resolve itself for short periods before returning. This pattern indicated a possible problem with the routing or switching configuration rather than a simple hardware failure.

As the incident progressed, it became clear that the intermittent nature of the packet loss was making it challenging to pinpoint the exact cause. The network team started by reviewing logs from the affected routers and switches, looking for any error messages or signs of congestion that could explain the packet loss. Despite these efforts, the root cause remained elusive, necessitating a deeper dive into the network's configuration and performance metrics.

The impact of this symptom was significant, as it affected a substantial portion of the network traffic. Users reported delays and timeouts when accessing certain resources, which in turn affected productivity and service availability. The network operations team worked closely with the monitoring team to gather more data on the nature of the packet loss, including the times of day it was most prevalent and any correlation with specific types of traffic.

Given the complexity of the issue and its impact on network performance, it was crucial to identify and resolve the root cause as quickly as possible. This involved a collaborative effort between different teams, each bringing their expertise to bear on the problem. From reviewing network configurations to analyzing traffic patterns, every potential lead was pursued to ensure that the incident could be fully resolved.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
Initial hypotheses focused on potential congestion or misconfiguration of the uplink. The team used tools like `dig` to check DNS resolution times and `tcpdump` to capture network traffic for analysis. One key command used in the diagnosis was:
```
tcpdump -i eth0 port 179
```
This helped in capturing BGP (Border Gateway Protocol) packets, which were crucial for understanding the routing decisions being made by the network devices.

Several hypotheses were explored and ruled out, including issues with DNS resolution, firewall rules blocking necessary traffic, and hardware failures. However, as the investigation deepened, it became clear that the issue was more closely related to the BGP peering session. The team considered the possibility of a misconfigured BGP session, which could lead to intermittent connectivity issues due to improper routing decisions.

## Resolution steps
1. Set BGP hold time to 30 seconds
2. Restart BGP service on router 10.1.1.1
3. Update BGP config file /etc/bgp.conf with new timer values
4. Verify BGP peering session with dig +short bgp 10.1.1.1

## Post-mortem note
The BGP peering session timed out due to misconfigured hold times. This root cause was identified after a thorough analysis of the network configuration and traffic patterns. The resolution steps effectively addressed the issue, restoring stable connectivity across the affected uplink. The incident highlighted the importance of regularly reviewing network configurations to prevent similar issues in the future.
