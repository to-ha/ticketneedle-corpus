# Major Incident — INC-2026-0015
* **Ticket ID:** INC-2026-0015
* **Reported:** 2026-04-18 22:14 UTC
* **Priority:** P2
* **Domain:** Database
* **Status:** Resolved

## Symptom
The incident began with reports of autovacuum canceling under sustained write load, which initially seemed like an isolated issue but quickly escalated into a broader problem affecting database performance. As the night progressed, the monitoring system caught increased latency in query execution times, particularly for those involving large, partitioned tables. This latency had a direct impact on user experience, with several business-critical applications relying on these databases for real-time data. The initial symptom of autovacuum canceling was observed through the database logs, where messages indicating the cancellation of autovacuum processes due to conflicting locks were logged repeatedly.

Further investigation revealed that this issue was not new but had been intermittently present over the past few weeks, albeit at a much lower frequency and severity. The recent increase in data volume, however, seemed to exacerbate the problem, making it more pronounced and frequent. The database's monitoring dashboard showed spikes in CPU usage and disk I/O, correlating with the times when autovacuum was canceled and query latency increased. Despite these clear indicators of a performance issue, the root cause remained elusive, prompting a deeper dive into the database configuration and recent changes.

The user impact was significant, with several reports of slow application performance and, in some cases, complete unavailability of certain features due to database timeouts. The business relied heavily on the real-time data provided by these databases, making any downtime or performance degradation critical. As the incident unfolded, it became clear that resolving the autovacuum issue and reducing query latency were paramount to restoring normal business operations.

The monitoring system played a crucial role in identifying the issue, although it did not catch the problem early enough to prevent its escalation. Alerts were triggered for high CPU usage and disk I/O, but these were initially thought to be related to a different issue. It wasn't until the database logs were manually reviewed that the autovacuum cancellation messages were found, pointing to the actual root cause. This highlighted a need for more specific monitoring rules tailored to the database's particular configuration and usage patterns.

As the incident response team delved deeper into the issue, they began to explore various tools and commands to diagnose the problem. The `psql` equivalent client was used extensively to execute queries and inspect database internals, while pgbouncer logs provided insights into connection pooling and potential bottlenecks. The `explain analyze` command was particularly useful in understanding query performance characteristics, helping to pinpoint where optimizations could be made.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
Initially, the team suspected that the issue might be related to insufficient resources (CPU, RAM, or disk space) given the recent increase in data volume. However, after reviewing the server's specifications and current utilization, it became clear that resources were not the limiting factor. The next hypothesis was that the autovacuum process itself was malfunctioning or not properly configured, leading to the cancellations under load. To investigate this, the team adjusted autovacuum settings to be more aggressive, but this did not resolve the issue.

Another hypothesis considered was that the partitioning scheme of the large tables was inefficient, leading to hotspots in disk I/O and thus causing the autovacuum to cancel. To explore this, the team executed the following command to analyze query performance on one of the large, partitioned tables:
```
psql -c 'EXPLAIN ANALYZE SELECT * FROM large_partitioned_table'
```
This provided valuable insights into how the database was executing queries on this table, highlighting potential bottlenecks in the partitioning scheme.

The team also explored the possibility that connection pooling was not adequately configured, leading to too many concurrent connections and thus increased contention for resources. However, after reviewing the pgbouncer configuration and logs, it was determined that connection pooling was not the primary cause of the issue.

As the diagnosis progressed, it became increasingly clear that the root cause was related to how the database handled its partitions under the increased load. The final hypothesis, which eventually led to the resolution, was that the table partitioning scheme was indeed the culprit, specifically due to its inability to efficiently handle the recent surge in data volume.

## Resolution steps
1. Set default_tablespace to 'pg_default' in postgresql.conf
2. Run VACUUM FULL on 'large_partitioned_table'
3. Adjust partitioning scheme to use 30-day intervals
4. Update pgbouncer.ini to increase max_client_conn to 500
5. Restart database service with -c config_file=/etc/db_config

## Post-mortem note
The table partitioning scheme was not adjusted for increased data volume. This oversight led to significant performance issues, including autovacuum cancellations and increased query latency, ultimately affecting business operations. In retrospect, regular reviews of database configuration in light of changing data volumes and patterns could have prevented this incident. Future actions will include implementing more dynamic partitioning schemes that can adapt to changes in data volume and implementing additional monitoring to catch similar issues before they escalate.
