## Symptom

The filesystem metadata server was observed to fail over every few hours, causing brief disruptions to storage services. This pattern was first noticed by monitoring tools, which alerted the team to the recurring issue. Upon further investigation, it became clear that the failovers were not due to any single point of failure but rather a periodic condition that affected the overall stability of the storage system. The exact cause of these failovers was not immediately apparent, requiring a deeper dive into system logs and metrics.

Initial observations suggested that the issue might be related to disk space or resource utilization, given the timing and nature of the failovers. However, without more specific data, it was challenging to pinpoint the exact root cause. The impact on users was minimal but noticeable, with some reporting delays in accessing certain files during the brief periods of failover.

The recurring nature of the issue hinted at a cyclic or threshold-based problem, possibly tied to how resources were being allocated or deallocated within the storage system. It was crucial to identify and address the underlying cause to prevent future occurrences and ensure the reliability of the storage infrastructure.

Given the specific focus on block storage volume snapshots, the team began to suspect that the issue might be related to snapshot management or the disk space allocated for snapshots. This suspicion would guide the initial diagnostic efforts.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses

The diagnostic process involved several hypotheses, each of which was tested and eventually ruled out based on evidence. One of the initial hypotheses was that the disk health might be a contributing factor, leading to the decision to run diagnostic tools on the disks. 
```
smartctl -a /dev/sda
```
This command provided valuable insights into the disk's condition but did not immediately reveal any critical issues that would cause the failovers.

Another hypothesis considered was that the issue might be related to the performance or load on the storage system, potentially triggering the failovers. Tools like `fio` were used to simulate load and test the system's response under stress, but this did not replicate the failover condition in a way that pointed to a clear root cause.

The team also explored the possibility of snapshot integrity issues, using `rsync --dry-run` to verify the consistency of snapshots without actually transferring data. While this helped in understanding the snapshot process, it did not directly lead to identifying the root cause of the failovers.

## Resolution steps

1. Run smartctl -a /dev/sda to check disk health
2. Set snapshot reserve to 20% on /dev/sda1
3. Execute fio --filename=/dev/sda1 --size=10G --readwrite=randrw
4. Verify snapshot integrity with rsync --dry-run -avz /mnt/src/ /mnt/dst/

## Post-mortem note

The block storage volume snapshot failed due to insufficient disk space. This root cause was ultimately identified through a combination of diagnostic steps and hypotheses testing, leading to the resolution that involved adjusting snapshot reserves and verifying disk health and snapshot integrity. The experience highlighted the importance of closely monitoring disk space allocation for snapshots and regularly reviewing system logs for early signs of potential issues.
