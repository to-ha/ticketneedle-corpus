# Major Incident — INC-2026-0075
* **Ticket ID:** INC-2026-0075
* **Reported:** 2026-01-31 23:08 UTC
* **Priority:** P2
* **Domain:** Database
* **Status:** Resolved

## Symptom
The incident began with reports of autovacuum canceling under sustained write load on the database. Initially, this was observed by the monitoring system, which alerted the team to potential issues with database performance. As the night progressed, users started to report slower query execution times and occasional timeouts when attempting to access certain tables. The most affected table was 'orders', which is a critical component of the application's functionality. Further investigation revealed that the autovacuum process was being canceled repeatedly due to transaction ID wraparound issues, suggesting a deeper problem with the database's configuration or data management.

The symptoms first appeared around 22:00 UTC, with the monitoring system picking up on the autovacuum cancellations. Initially, these were sporadic and did not seem to impact user experience significantly. However, as the volume of writes to the 'orders' table increased, the frequency and duration of these cancellations also increased, eventually leading to noticeable performance degradation for users. The team was alerted at 23:08 UTC, prompting an immediate investigation into the cause of the issue.

The user and business impact of this incident was significant, as any delay or failure in accessing or updating orders directly affects revenue and customer satisfaction. The application's performance metrics, such as query latency and error rates, began to deteriorate, indicating a problem that needed urgent attention. The monitoring system played a crucial role in identifying the issue early, but it was clear that a more detailed analysis of the database's state and recent changes was necessary to diagnose the root cause.

As the team delved deeper into the issue, they utilized tools like `iostat` to monitor disk usage and `explain analyze` with `psql` to understand query performance and potential bottlenecks. These diagnostic efforts aimed to pinpoint whether the problem lay with the database's configuration, the application's query patterns, or perhaps an issue with the underlying storage.

Given the focus on the 'orders' table and the symptoms observed, the team hypothesized that the issue might be related to how data was being managed within this table, particularly considering its high volume of writes and potential for growth. The fact that autovacuum was canceling suggested issues with transaction IDs or possibly even disk space, but these were just initial guesses that required further investigation.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
The team initially suspected that the issue might be related to disk space or configuration settings that were not optimized for the current workload. They checked disk usage and found that while there was sufficient free space, the autovacuum process was still being canceled, which suggested that the problem might be more related to transaction ID wraparound or locking issues rather than simple resource constraints.

Another hypothesis considered was that the queries themselves were inefficient, leading to prolonged transactions that interfered with autovacuum. To investigate this, the team used `explain analyze` to examine query plans and identify any potential bottlenecks. However, the queries in question seemed well-optimized, and there was no clear indication that they were the primary cause of the cancellations.

Further investigation led to exploring the database's partitioning scheme, particularly for the 'orders' table, given its high write volume. Before diving into complex configurations, the team wanted to understand the query execution plans better, so they ran:
```
psql -c 'EXPLAIN ANALYZE SELECT * FROM orders'
```
This helped in identifying how the database was accessing the 'orders' table and whether there were any obvious issues with the current partitioning scheme that could be contributing to the performance problems.

The team also considered the possibility of locking conflicts or deadlocks, given the high concurrency of writes to the 'orders' table. However, an examination of the database logs did not reveal significant evidence of such issues at the time of the autovacuum cancellations.

It wasn't until the team started to investigate the partitioning scheme more closely that they began to suspect the actual root cause. The 'orders' table was partitioned by month, but the recent increase in data volume had not been accounted for in the partitioning strategy. This oversight led to an imbalance in data distribution across partitions, potentially causing the autovacuum process to fail under the sustained write load.

## Resolution steps
1. Set default_tablespace to 'pg_default' in postgresql.conf
2. Run VACUUM FULL on table 'orders' with 1024MB of free space
3. Adjust partitioning scheme for 'orders' to use 12 monthly intervals
4. Update pg_partition_config to reflect new interval range
5. Restart database service with --reload-config flag

## Post-mortem note
The table partitioning scheme was not adjusted for increased data volume. This root cause, once identified, was addressed through the resolution steps outlined above, which included adjusting the partitioning scheme and ensuring the database configuration was optimized for the current workload. The incident highlighted the importance of regularly reviewing database configurations and adapting them to changes in data volume and usage patterns. Following this incident, the team has scheduled a review of all critical tables' partitioning schemes to prevent similar issues from arising in the future. Additionally, automated monitoring for partition size and data growth rates will be implemented to provide early warnings for potential issues, allowing for proactive adjustments before they impact performance.
