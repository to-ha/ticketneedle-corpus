## Symptom
The CI pipelines began to stall consistently on the same stage across all repositories, indicating a potential issue with the internal service discovery mechanism. This symptom first appeared around 14:15 UTC, with the first few pipeline runs completing successfully before the stalls started occurring. As the issue evolved, it became clear that the problem was not specific to any particular repository or pipeline configuration, suggesting a more fundamental issue with the underlying infrastructure. The user and business impact was significant, as the stalled pipelines prevented the deployment of new code changes and hindered the development team's ability to deliver updates. Monitoring caught the initial pipeline stalls, but it took some time to realize that this was not an isolated issue with a single pipeline or repository.

The monitoring system alerted the team to the high failure rate of pipeline runs, which initially suggested a problem with the pipeline configuration or the build process itself. However, as the team dug deeper, they realized that the issue was more related to the service discovery mechanism, which relied on internal DNS for resolving service names to IP addresses. The fact that all pipelines were stalling at the same stage pointed towards a common dependency or service that was not being resolved correctly.

Further investigation into the monitoring data revealed that the DNS resolution times had increased significantly around the time the pipeline stalls started occurring. This suggested that there might be an issue with the DNS server or the cache, which could be causing the service discovery mechanism to fail. The team decided to focus on the internal DNS and service discovery as the primary area of investigation.

The CI pipeline stalls were also causing a ripple effect, impacting other dependent services and systems. The development team was unable to deploy new changes, which meant that fixes for existing issues were not being applied, and new features were not being released. This had a direct impact on the business, as users were not getting the updates they needed, and the development team was not able to respond quickly to changing requirements.

As the issue persisted, the team realized that they needed to escalate the problem to get more expertise and resources involved. The L1 Platform support team was initially engaged, but as the issue proved to be more complex, it was escalated to the L2 Platform engineer, and eventually to the L3 Platform lead and the On-call manager.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
The team initially suspected that the issue might be related to the pipeline configuration or the build process itself. However, as they dug deeper, they considered several other hypotheses, including issues with the internal DNS server, problems with the service discovery mechanism, and potential network connectivity issues.

One hypothesis was that the DNS server might be experiencing high latency or packet loss, causing the service discovery mechanism to fail. To investigate this, the team used `tcpdump` to capture DNS traffic and analyze the response times. However, the evidence suggested that the DNS server was responding quickly and correctly, ruling out this hypothesis.

Another hypothesis was that the service discovery mechanism might be incorrectly configured, causing it to fail to resolve service names to IP addresses. The team used `flag-history` to review the configuration changes made to the service discovery mechanism, but found no evidence of recent changes that could have caused the issue.

The team also suspected that there might be a problem with the network connectivity between the pipeline runners and the DNS server. To investigate this, they used `dig` to test the DNS resolution from different locations, but found that the issue was not specific to any particular location or network path.

As the team continued to investigate, they considered the hypothesis that the internal DNS cache might be overloaded or incorrectly configured, causing the service discovery mechanism to fail. To test this, they used the following command:
```
dig +trace example.service
```
This command helped to reveal that the DNS cache was indeed experiencing issues, which led the team to focus on the DNS cache as the primary cause of the problem.

The team also used `registry-gc` to analyze the DNS records and identify any potential issues with stale or expired records. This helped to rule out other hypotheses and narrow down the focus to the DNS cache.

After considering several hypotheses and gathering evidence, the team finally identified the root cause of the issue: the internal DNS cache had become overloaded due to incorrect TTL settings, causing the service discovery mechanism to fail.

## Resolution steps
1. Restart dns-cache with flag --max-ttl 300
2. Set dns.cache.size to 1024 in /etc/dns/config
3. Run registry-gc with --dry-run to identify stale records
4. Remove expired records from dns.db using broker-admin
5. Verify dns resolution with dig +short example.service

## Post-mortem note
The internal DNS cache became overloaded due to incorrect ttl settings. This issue was missed initially because the team was focused on other potential causes, such as pipeline configuration issues or network connectivity problems. In hindsight, the team should have considered the DNS cache as a potential cause earlier, given the symptoms and the evidence from the monitoring data. To prevent similar issues in the future, the team will review the DNS cache configuration and ensure that the TTL settings are correctly configured. Additionally, the team will implement more robust monitoring and alerting for the DNS cache to detect potential issues before they cause service disruptions. The incident highlights the importance of considering all possible causes when investigating complex issues and the need for thorough monitoring and alerting to detect potential problems early.
