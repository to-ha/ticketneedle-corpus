* **Domain:** Containers / Kubernetes
* **Status:** Resolved

## Symptom
The incident began with reports of pods failing to become Ready after being scaled up by the Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA). Initially, the issue seemed intermittent, but as time progressed, it became clear that none of the newly scaled pods were transitioning to a Ready state. Monitoring tools indicated an increase in pod creation events without corresponding increases in ready pod counts. This discrepancy suggested a problem with the pod lifecycle management. Further investigation revealed that the kubelet on node-123 was not properly syncing with etcd, which led to a cascade of issues affecting pod readiness.

As the incident evolved, the impact on users became more pronounced. Applications dependent on the scaled pods began experiencing errors and timeouts due to the lack of available instances. The user community reported frustration with the service degradation, prompting an urgent response from the operations team. The initial response included attempts to manually intervene in the pod scaling process and verify the health of node-123.

The monitoring system caught the initial surge in scale-up events but did not immediately alert on the failure of pods to become Ready. It wasn't until the user community began reporting issues that the full extent of the problem became apparent. Tools like stern were used to inspect the logs of the pods and the kubelet, revealing clues about the nature of the issue. However, the root cause remained elusive, necessitating a deeper dive into the Kubernetes components involved.

The focus on node-123 and its interaction with etcd guided further troubleshooting efforts. Commands like `kubectl describe` were used to inspect pod and node statuses, while `etcdctl` was employed to query etcd directly about its view of the cluster state. Despite these efforts, the symptoms persisted, indicating a need for more targeted diagnostic approaches.

As the incident continued, the team recognized the need for a methodical approach to diagnosing the issue. This involved breaking down the pod lifecycle into its constituent parts and examining each for potential failures. The involvement of etcd and the kubelet suggested that the problem might lie in their communication or in the configuration of these components.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
Initial hypotheses centered around issues with the pod scaling mechanism itself or problems with the network connectivity between nodes. The team suspected that there might be a configuration error in the HPA or in the pod definitions that was preventing them from becoming Ready. Diagnostic steps included verifying the HPA configuration and checking for any recent changes to pod templates.

Another hypothesis considered was that there was an issue with the Docker runtime on node-123, preventing containers from starting correctly. This led to attempts to restart Docker and verify its configuration. However, upon inspection, Docker seemed to be functioning as expected, leading the team to rule out this hypothesis.

The team also explored the possibility of a resource constraint on node-123, such as insufficient CPU or memory, that could prevent pods from becoming Ready. Commands like `kubectl top` were used to monitor resource usage, but no evidence of resource exhaustion was found.

Further investigation led to suspicions about the health of etcd, given its critical role in Kubernetes cluster management. The team considered the possibility of etcd being unavailable or improperly configured, which would disrupt the kubelet's ability to manage pods. To diagnose this, the following command was used:
```
etcdctl member list --cluster=docker-localhost:2379
```
This step provided insight into etcd's view of the cluster and helped identify potential issues with its membership or connectivity.

As the diagnosis progressed, the team began to focus more closely on the interaction between the kubelet on node-123 and etcd. Evidence suggested that there might be a quorum issue affecting etcd, which would explain the kubelet's failure to sync and the subsequent pod readiness issues. This hypothesis was explored through adjustments to etcd's configuration and verification of its quorum state.

The actual root cause emerged after a thorough review of etcd's logs and configuration, coupled with an understanding of the kubelet's behavior in response to etcd's state. It became clear that the issue was not with the pods themselves or the scaling mechanism but rather with the underlying infrastructure supporting Kubernetes.

## Resolution steps
1. Restart kubelet on node-123 with --v=4 flag
2. Set etcd-quorum-learning-duration to 10s in /etc/kubernetes/config
3. Run etcdctl member list to verify node-123 connectivity
4. Increase log level to 5 in /var/lib/kubelet/config.yaml
5. Verify node-123 certificate expiration with openssl

## Post-mortem note
The kubelet on node-123 failed to sync with etcd due to a quorum issue. This root cause was initially missed due to the focus on symptoms related to pod scaling and readiness, rather than the underlying cluster management infrastructure. In retrospect, earlier inspection of etcd's health and configuration might have led to a quicker resolution. Follow-up actions include implementing additional monitoring for etcd's quorum state and enhancing the diagnostics toolkit to better capture issues related to Kubernetes component interactions. Furthermore, the incident highlighted the importance of comprehensive logging and the need for detailed documentation of cluster configurations to facilitate rapid troubleshooting in the future.
