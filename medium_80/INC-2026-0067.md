## Symptom
The incident began with reports of pods stuck in CrashLoopBackOff after a node reboot. This symptom was first observed by monitoring tools, which alerted the on-call team to investigate potential issues with container runtime. Upon further inspection, it became clear that the problem was not isolated to a single pod or node, but rather a widespread issue affecting multiple pods across different nodes. The pods in question were unable to restart successfully, leading to a significant impact on service availability.

As the investigation continued, it was noted that the node reboot had been scheduled as part of routine maintenance. However, the unexpected behavior of the pods suggested that there might be an underlying issue with the container runtime configuration or the node's ability to manage pod restarts. The symptom complex revolved around the CrashLoopBackOff status, indicating that the containers were crashing or failing to start, and the system was unable to successfully restart them.

The impact of this symptom was significant, as it affected the overall availability and reliability of the services hosted on the affected nodes. Users began to report errors and timeouts when attempting to access these services, highlighting the need for a swift resolution to the issue. The on-call team recognized the urgency and began to escalate the issue through the appropriate channels.

Given the nature of the symptom, the initial focus was on understanding why the pods were failing to restart after the node reboot. This involved reviewing logs, checking container runtime configurations, and assessing the health of the nodes themselves. However, as the diagnosis phase progressed, it became apparent that the root cause might be more nuanced, requiring a deeper dive into the container runtime and its interactions with the node and pod management systems.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
Initial hypotheses focused on potential misconfigurations in the container runtime or issues with the node's ability to restart pods. The team considered the possibility of resource constraints, network connectivity problems, or even version compatibility issues between different components of the Kubernetes setup. However, as each hypothesis was tested, it became clear that the issue was more closely related to the logging and restart mechanisms within the container runtime.

One key command used during the diagnosis was:
```
crictl info --log-level=debug
```
This helped in understanding the detailed state of the container runtime and identifying any potential issues with logging or resource allocation that could be causing the pods to crash or fail to restart.

Despite the efforts to pinpoint the exact cause, the team had to consider multiple hypotheses before narrowing down to the root cause. This involved ruling out several potential causes, including network issues, resource limitations, and configuration errors, based on evidence gathered from logs, system metrics, and direct inspection of the container runtime state.

## Resolution steps
1. Restart container runtime with --log-level=debug flag
2. Set max-container-log-size to 100MB in /etc/container/config.json
3. Run crictl logs --id container_id to verify log rotation
4. Update etcdctl timeout to 5s in /etc/etcd/etcd.conf
5. Apply helm chart with updated container runtime configuration

## Post-mortem note
The container runtime crashed due to excessive logging. This root cause was identified after a thorough diagnosis that involved testing several hypotheses and gathering evidence from various sources, including logs and system metrics. The resolution steps effectively addressed the issue by adjusting logging settings, verifying log rotation, and updating relevant configurations to prevent similar incidents in the future. The incident highlighted the importance of monitoring logging levels and ensuring that container runtime configurations are optimized for the specific needs of the services being hosted.
