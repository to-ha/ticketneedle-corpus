## Symptom
The incident began with a noticeable increase in the message broker queue depth, which grew unbounded over time. This symptom was first observed by the monitoring system at 17:20 UTC, triggering an alert that was escalated to the on-call team. As the queue depth continued to increase, it started to impact the overall performance of the platform, leading to delays in message processing and eventually causing timeouts for dependent services. The growth of the queue depth was steady and consistent, indicating a potential issue with the internal DNS resolution for service discovery, which is critical for the message broker's operation.

Further investigation into the monitoring data revealed that the issue was not limited to a single service or node but was rather a platform-wide problem. The metrics platform showed a significant increase in DNS query failures, which correlated with the growth of the message broker queue depth. This suggested that the root cause of the issue might be related to the internal DNS infrastructure. The team also noticed that the affected services were all relying on the internal DNS for service discovery, which further supported this hypothesis.

The user impact of the incident was substantial, as it affected the processing of critical messages across the platform. The business relied heavily on the timely delivery of these messages, and the delays caused by the incident had significant consequences. The incident highlighted the importance of the internal DNS infrastructure and the need for robust monitoring and failover mechanisms to prevent such incidents in the future.

As the incident evolved, the team observed that the DNS query failures were not limited to a specific domain or service but were rather widespread across the platform. This indicated a potential issue with the DNS cache or the underlying infrastructure. The team decided to investigate further, focusing on the DNS cache and its configuration.

The monitoring system played a crucial role in detecting the incident, as it provided real-time data on the message broker queue depth and DNS query failures. However, the team realized that the monitoring setup could be improved to provide more detailed insights into the DNS infrastructure and its performance. This would enable the team to detect similar incidents earlier and respond more effectively.

## Diagnosis steps and hypotheses
The team considered several hypotheses during the diagnosis process. Initially, they suspected that the issue might be related to a recent change in the DNS configuration. To investigate this, they reviewed the configuration files and checked for any recent updates. However, they found no evidence of any changes that could have caused the issue.

Another hypothesis was that the problem might be related to a network connectivity issue, affecting the DNS queries. The team used tools like `tcpdump` to capture DNS traffic and analyze it, but they found no evidence of network-related problems.

The team also suspected that the issue might be related to the DNS cache size or configuration. To investigate this, they ran the command:
```
kubectl get deployments -o yaml | grep dns.cache-size
```
This command helped them understand the current DNS cache configuration and identify potential issues. However, the output did not reveal any obvious problems with the cache size or configuration.

As the team continued to investigate, they considered the hypothesis that the issue might be related to the registry garbage collection process. They ran the `registry-gc` tool with the `--dry-run` option to simulate the garbage collection process and see if it would resolve the issue. However, this did not have any effect on the DNS query failures.

The team also suspected that the problem might be related to the vault configuration or the DNS certificate. They checked the vault logs and found no issues with the certificate or configuration. However, they decided to update the vault with a new DNS certificate to rule out any potential problems.

After trying several different approaches and ruling out various hypotheses, the team finally discovered that the internal DNS cache was not being updated correctly due to an incorrect TTL setting. This realization led them to focus on the DNS cache configuration and ultimately identify the root cause of the incident.

## Resolution steps
1. Restart dns-cache with --ttl=300
2. Set dns.cache-size to 1024 in /etc/dns/config
3. Run registry-gc with --dry-run=false
4. Update vault with new DNS certificate at /vault/secrets/dns
5. Verify DNS resolution with dig +short example.service

## Post-mortem note
The internal DNS cache expired due to incorrect ttl setting. This issue was missed initially because the team focused on other potential causes, such as network connectivity problems or recent configuration changes. In retrospect, the team should have investigated the DNS cache configuration earlier, as it was a critical component of the platform's service discovery mechanism. To prevent similar incidents in the future, the team will review and update the monitoring setup to provide more detailed insights into the DNS infrastructure and its performance. Additionally, they will implement more robust testing and validation procedures for DNS-related changes to ensure that such issues are caught earlier.
